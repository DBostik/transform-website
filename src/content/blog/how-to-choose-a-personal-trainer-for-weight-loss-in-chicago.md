## Weight loss coaching is its own skill

Plenty of trainers can make you tired. Far fewer can make you lighter a year from now.

That is because the workout is only one piece of losing weight, and honestly, not the biggest piece. What you eat matters more than what you lift. What you do the other 165 hours of the week matters more than the hours you spend with a trainer. A trainer who hands you a hard workout and a high five, then disappears until your next appointment, is leaving most of the job undone.

A real weight loss trainer manages three things at once: your training, your nutrition, and your consistency. If any of the three is missing from their pitch, keep looking.

One more piece of context before the questions. A sustainable pace for most people is about [1 to 2 pounds a week](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-weight-growth/losing-weight/index.html), and people who lose at that gradual pace are more likely to keep the weight off. Hold every promise you hear during a sales pitch against that number.

## Seven questions to ask before you hire anyone

Take this list with you, whether you are touring a big gym, meeting an independent trainer, or sitting down with us.

**1. How will you handle my nutrition?** This is the question that exposes the most trainers. "Just eat clean" is not an answer. Neither is a supplement catalog. You want to hear something specific: how they assess what you eat now, what guidance you get, and how it adjusts when your progress stalls. At our studio every plan includes nutrition guidance because we have never once seen a client out-train a bad diet.

**2. What happens between sessions?** Weight loss is decided on Tuesday night at 9pm, not during your Thursday appointment. Ask whether you can reach your trainer between sessions, whether anyone checks on you, and what happens when you go quiet for a week. Daily support is the single most underrated thing you can buy.

**3. How do you measure progress?** The right answer involves an assessment at the start and regular measurements after: weight trends, measurements, photos, strength numbers, how your clothes fit. If the only measuring tool is the bathroom scale, plateaus will get misread and you will get discouraged for no reason.

**4. Have you coached people like me?** Like you specifically: your age, your starting point, your injuries, your schedule. A trainer who mostly preps 25-year-old athletes may be a poor fit for a 45-year-old with a desk job and a cranky knee. Ask to see results. Any established trainer should be able to point to real client outcomes, the way we can point to our [success stories](/success-stories/).

**5. What is your plan for when I plateau?** Everyone stalls eventually. The good trainers expect it and have a process: review the food log, check sleep and stress, adjust the calorie target or the training. The bad ones just tell you to try harder. If they have no answer prepared, they have not coached many people through it.

**6. How long are your sessions, and why?** There is no single right answer here, but the trainer should have a reason beyond "that's the industry standard." We deliberately run 30-minute sessions because focused, well-programmed half hours fit real Chicago schedules and get repeated week after week, and consistency is what produces results. A trainer who needs an hour should be able to tell you what fills it. If the honest answer is rest periods and chit-chat, you are paying for filler.

**7. What does it cost, in writing?** Pricing should not require three meetings to discover. Ask for the number, the package terms, and the cancellation policy up front. We publish our prices, and we broke down what everyone else in the city charges in our guide to [personal trainer costs in Chicago](/blog/how-much-does-a-personal-trainer-cost-in-chicago/), so you know the going rates before any sales conversation.

## Red flags that should end the conversation

Some things are not just yellow flags to weigh. In our experience they are reasons to walk out.

- Guaranteed results by a date. "Lose 20 pounds by your reunion" is marketing, not coaching. Bodies do not sign contracts.
- Crash diets, cleanses, or detoxes as the starting plan. The faster the promised loss, the faster the rebound.
- A supplement pitch in the first meeting. The markup is the motive.
- No assessment before training starts. If they will write your program without learning your history, injuries, and starting point, the program was written before you arrived.
- Long contracts you cannot exit. Confident trainers do not need a 12-month lock to keep clients. Month to month means they have to earn your business every month, which is how it should be.
- Every client doing the same workout. Watch the floor for ten minutes. If the 60-year-old beginner and the 28-year-old marathoner are doing identical circuits, the word "personal" is decorative.
- Soreness used as proof. Being wrecked for three days is not a result. It is usually the reason people quit.

## Where trainers work in Chicago, and why it matters

The same city gives you four very different ways to be coached. None is wrong, but they fit different people.

Big gym floors put trainers steps from your existing membership, which is convenient. Quality varies enormously, turnover is high, and the trainer often splits attention across a loud, crowded floor. Independent trainers who rent space can be excellent, just do extra homework on experience and reviews since there is no studio standard behind them. Private studios like ours trade the crowd for focus: one trainer, one client, a quiet room, and nobody waiting for your squat rack. And online coaching has matured into a real option if your schedule or location makes in-person hard. We offer it ourselves through our [online training](/online-training/) program, and it works best for self-starters who mainly need programming and accountability rather than hands-on form coaching.
