If there is one nutrition change we bring up with almost every new client, it is protein. Not because it is magic, but because most people eat less of it than they think, and getting enough makes the rest of the plan easier.

Here is what protein does, roughly how much you need, and how to get there without living on plain chicken breast.

## What protein actually does

Protein is one of the three macronutrients, along with carbohydrates and fat. If that term is new to you, our post on [what macros are](/blog/what-are-macros/) covers the basics.

Your body breaks protein down into amino acids and uses them to build and repair tissue: muscle, skin, hair, and the collagen that gives your bones and tendons their structure. Amino acids also go into enzymes, antibodies and some hormones.

Your body does not store protein the way it stores fat, so you need a steady supply from food every day. For people who train, two benefits matter most: fullness and holding on to muscle.

## Why protein helps you feel full

Protein is the most filling of the three macronutrients. A breakfast built around eggs or Greek yogurt tends to hold you longer than a bagel or a bowl of cereal with similar calories. Your body also burns slightly more energy digesting protein than carbs or fat, though the effect is small.

This is the practical reason we push protein for weight loss. When you are eating a bit less than you burn, hunger is usually what ends the plan. Meals that keep you satisfied make it far easier to stick with.

Fiber helps too, so pair protein with beans, vegetables, oats, whole grains or fruit. Pastries, chips and cookies are easy to overeat and do little for fullness, which is why swapping a few for protein snacks is often the first change we suggest.

## Why it helps you keep muscle while you lose fat

When you lose weight, some of what comes off can be muscle instead of fat. Strength training plus enough protein is the best way to tilt that loss toward fat. Keeping muscle keeps you strong and helps you look firmer instead of just smaller.

And no, eating more protein will not make you bulky. Building a lot of muscle takes years of focused training and usually eating more calories than you burn. Protein on its own does not do it. Our [guide to putting on muscle](/blog/why-am-i-gaining-weight-the-guide-to-putting-on-muscle-and-how-it-helps-burn-fat/) explains why a little muscle is one of the best things you can add while losing fat.

## How much protein do you need?

The baseline recommendation for adults is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. That covers basic needs for mostly sedentary people, not regular training.

For people who exercise regularly, sports nutrition guidelines commonly cite about 1.2 to 2.0 grams per kilogram per day. In pounds, that works out to roughly 0.5 to 0.9 grams per pound of body weight. People trying to lose fat while keeping muscle often do well toward the higher end of that range.

For a 150 pound person, that is roughly 80 to 135 grams a day.

Treat that as general information, not a prescription. Your right number depends on your size, your goal, how hard you train and what you actually like to eat. If you have kidney disease or another medical condition, check with your doctor before increasing your protein.

Spreading it across your day helps. Roughly 20 to 40 grams per meal is a practical target for most adults, and it is much easier than trying to catch up at dinner.

## Where to get it

Protein does not have to mean meat. Here are common sources with rough amounts. Numbers vary by brand and preparation, so check labels when you can.

**Animal sources**

- Chicken or turkey breast, 3 ounces cooked: about 26 grams
- Lean beef or pork, 3 ounces cooked: about 22 to 25 grams
- Salmon or canned tuna, 3 ounces: about 20 grams
- Plain nonfat Greek yogurt, 6 ounces: about 17 grams
- Cottage cheese, half a cup: about 12 to 14 grams
- Milk, 1 cup: about 8 grams
- Eggs: about 6 grams each

**Plant sources**

- Lentils, 1 cup cooked: about 18 grams
- Edamame, 1 cup shelled: about 18 grams
- Black beans or chickpeas, 1 cup cooked: about 12 to 15 grams
- Pumpkin seeds, 1 ounce: about 8 grams
- Almonds or peanuts, 1 ounce: about 6 to 7 grams
- Tofu and tempeh: varies by type, so check the label

Vegetables like broccoli and spinach, and grains like quinoa, have some protein, just not much per serving. If you eat mostly plant-based, lean on beans, lentils, soy foods, nuts and seeds, and mix sources through the day.

Protein powder can fill a gap on busy days. It is a convenience, not a requirement. We compare the main types in our post on [protein powder options](/blog/what-is-the-best-protein-powder-for-women/).

## Easy high-protein snacks

Snacks are the easiest place to add protein, because that is where most people default to crackers or chips. A few ideas:

- Greek yogurt with berries
- Cottage cheese with fruit, or with sliced tomatoes and black pepper
- Hard-boiled eggs with a little hot sauce
- Egg muffins made ahead: eggs and chopped vegetables baked in a muffin tin
- Canned tuna or salmon on whole grain crackers
- Edamame with a pinch of salt
- Roasted chickpeas
- Jerky, checking the sodium and sugar
- Apple or celery with peanut or almond butter

On protein bars: some are solid, and plenty are closer to candy bars with protein added. Read the label for added sugar and how much protein you get for the calories.

## Where to start

You do not need to overhaul everything. Pick one meal, usually breakfast, and build it around a protein source. Once that feels automatic, do the same with your afternoon snack.
